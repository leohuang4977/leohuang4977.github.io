A decision pipeline for my own job search, with me at the last step.

Every week, a pipeline I designed reads the career sites on my watchlist and scores each new posting against rules I set. Claude vets the shortlist and fills applications up to the Submit button. I press Submit myself. It also turned up the finding that changed my search: in about 7,300 postings in Canada or remote, not one had a behavioral-science title.

01

Why I built it

In August 2026 I started a job search aimed straight at employers: a watchlist of companies in the Toronto area and remote across Canada, read on their own career sites, with no aggregators in between. Two things made that hard to do by hand.

The first was names. The work I do hadn't gone away, but it was being posted under titles that a search for "UX researcher" would never match. The second was volume. The first read of the forty-company watchlist returned 12,503 postings, and they didn't sit still. Across 602 postings on Workday sites, the median one closed 12 days after the pipeline first saw it.

Reading all of that every week, under the wrong names, was never going to work. I wanted a machine to do the reading and leave the decisions to me, with rules in between that I could inspect and change.

02

What I designed

The result is a weekly chain of eight stages. Every stage has a written rule and a guardrail, and each one belongs to a single actor: a script, Claude, or me. Scripts fetch and score. Claude reads where a rule can't decide, and drafts what I would otherwise write. Anything that commits me waits for me.

Each run starts on my Windows machine, which fetches the postings. Claude scheduled tasks then score them and build application packets, and Claude in Chrome handles assisted form filling that stops before Submit. The system never submits an application, and it stores no credentials.

I built it with Claude Code between August and October, across 71 commits and about 30 logged sessions. I set the rules and made the calls. Claude wrote and tested the code, and each working session now ends with a written handoff that marks every claim verified or unverified, so the next one starts from facts instead of memory.

03

The decisions built into it

Scores come from code, not judgment

In the first runs, Claude scored postings by judgment, and identical postings drifted between runs. So the rubric moved into code, checked by a test suite that grew from 23 real postings in August to 303 cases by October. A promising title whose description hasn't been fetched is floored at 3 and tagged, because missing evidence is not the same as a low score.

New role families start on probation

A new family of titles starts on probation unless I graduate it on day one. On probation it gets its own section in the weekly digest and is never picked automatically, and a noisy family can be deleted without touching the rest of the rubric.

Some matches are false friends

Some jobs share a word with mine and nothing else. On Canada's Job Bank, a search for behavioral scientists returned only applied behavior analysis therapy roles. False friends like that score 0 before anything else is read. Work I've ruled out for myself earns no title points, and the judge turns away anything from that list that slips through.

Caps, and a judge that can only say no

The pipeline picks at most 30 new roles in any seven days, with no more than 3 open at one employer. Then Claude judges every pick against its full description and keeps it only when the actual work is something I could credibly do. A rejected pick gets a one-line reason and never comes back, and its slot refills from the rubric, not from the model. In the October 20 run the judge sent back 18 of 42 picks.

What only I can change

Nothing writes to the watchlist, to employer priorities or to a status that records my decision unless I say so, and the pipeline's own picks carry a separate status, so it never decides in my name. Rules change only by dated amendments I authorize. Two of the biggest changes also went through a review by a separate agent before commit. On September 28 that review found nine problems, and seven were fixed before the change went in.

04

What it found

On October 21, 2026, the ledger held about 34,900 postings first seen since August 5. The rules had filtered out most of them, and 25 sat on the shortlist.

The finding I use most is about the market. A read of the ledger on September 28 covered about 7,300 distinct postings in Canada or remote, at 62 employers, first seen between August 6 and September 22. None carried a behavioral-science title.

The work was there under other names. Experimentation titles had joined the rubric in early September. Another 46 postings at watchlist employers sat under journey, decisioning, AI-enablement and people-analytics titles, and every one had scored 3 or lower because the rubric gave those titles no points.

So the search changed. I added five role families for decision design, meaning work that studies how people decide and then designs and tests the steps they move through, and widened a sixth for people analytics. The judge now keeps that work. It is also why this site leads with decisions and journeys rather than UX research.

05

What broke, and what I changed

Three failures shaped the design more than any plan did. None of them announced itself.

The cloud couldn't reach the job boards

The first scheduled scan, on August 31, reached 0 of 34 boards. Every request died at the cloud sandbox's proxy, even to sites that have nothing to do with jobs, and a third sandbox gave the same answer. So I split the run along the network line: Windows Task Scheduler on my machine fetches and caches every description, and the Claude task scores offline from that cache. The fetch now checks the network before it writes anything, because the failed run had logged 34 failures against boards that were fine. On September 4 the split design ran end to end and brought in 3,856 new postings.

Failures that made no noise

On August 31 I also found the pipeline had been silent for 25 days. Its scheduled task was gone, and its scoring had been blind: none of 12,842 postings had description text, so no posting could score above 3, one short of the top tier. Twenty-two roles that fit what I wanted had been filed away as low scores. Days later, three runs stalled on a permission prompt with nobody there to answer it, and in early October I found the scoring task had been off for two weeks while the fetch kept running.

The fixes share one idea: the system has to report on itself. A state probe prints the real state in one command, scan lag and any fetch in flight included. Long scripts print timestamped heartbeats. A watchdog checks that every step of the chain left its files, and the scorer refuses any fetch that isn't from today.

A board that seemed to empty overnight

On September 4, one employer's board adapter returned 5 postings where the first read had found 3,753. Had absence meant closure, thousands of live roles would have closed in one run. A mass-disappearance guard caught it and held back about 7,600 closures across 19 employers. Then the guard stuck the other way, and by September 9 it was holding postings open at 13 employers after they had come down. The fix was an aging rule: a posting now closes only after it goes missing from three trustworthy reads in a row.

06

What it shows

The pipeline is a small version of the work I'm looking for. A decision people make over and over gets written rules, and a person stays where judgment and accountability belong. Claude reads at a scale I can't, and its authority is narrow on purpose: it can turn a pick down but never add one, and it stops at Submit. When something breaks, the system says so before I have to ask.
